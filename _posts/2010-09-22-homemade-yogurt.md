<h5><span style="color: #444444;">From August until tomorrow, Ari and I have been subjects in a clinical research study at the Cleveland Clinic.  We are <span style="text-decoration: line-through;">too embarrassed</span> not at liberty to disclose the details of the study (don't worry, we were compensated for our suffering).  One thing I can say:  because it contains probiotics, we were not allowed to eat yogurt, one of our singular pleasures.  So. I've made a fresh batch in anticipation of our colonic liberation.</span></h5>
<h5><span style="color: #444444;">In many countries, making yogurt at home is standard.  My grandmother mixes a little yogurt into a vessel of heated milk, and the reliably warm Indian weather sets the yogurt overnight on the countertop. </span></h5>
<h5><span style="color: #444444;">In the more meteorologically fickle American midwest, we have to resort to the yogurt maker.  It's a glorified incubator, as unitask as it comes.  But it keeps the culture bacteria at a constant temperature so that it can fission forth and prosper.  The yogurt it produces is tasty, economical, and gut friendly.  I bought a <a href="http://i.acdn.us/image/A9276/927643/300_927643.jpg">Salton yogurt maker</a> for $18 last year-- it puts out 5 single servings of creamy yogurt per half-quart of 2% milk.  See below for a chart on how to get the level of tang that you desire. </span><span style="color: #444444;">Yes: it only makes plain yogurt.  My favorite post-production additions are black raspberry jam or brown sugar in the morning.  Stay tuned for Ari's post on Sunday - she has an even better use for it.</span></h5>

Ingredients (mostly hardware, really)
<ul>
	<li>A yogurt maker.  Some people swear that the oven light or a thermos will set the yogurt just as well, but...meh.</li>
	<li>A thermometer.  The Salton comes with one.</li>
	<li>2 pints (1/2 quart) of milk.  I use 2%, Vitamin D milk may come out even creamier.</li>
	<li>3 tablespoons of your starter yogurt (I use <a href="http://erivandairy.com/products.aspx"><span style="color: #444444;">Erivan</span></a> yogurt as my starter, but you can use any yogurt with active cultures listed in the ingredients).</li>
</ul>
<h5><span style="color: #444444;">Heat the milk on the stove in a pot big enough to leave a few inches between the surface of the milk and the rim.  When the milk starts to simmer, turn off the heat and place the thermometer into the milk.  When the milk falls to a temperature of 110F, thoroughly mix the yogurt starter into the milk.  I usually make the mistake of forgetting about the milk and it scalds and curdles a little bit.  To avoid the curdles from getting into my yogurt, I pour the yogurt-milk through a fine-mesh sieve into each yogurt cup.  Cap the cups and place them in your yogurt maker.  Leave the yogurt to set for your desired number of hours (I usually start it at 11pm and stop it at 7am).  Remove the yogurt from the yogurt maker and store it in the fridge for at least 2 hours before consuming.</span></h5>
